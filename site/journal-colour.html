Colour Comes Last

Ayushi · 4 min read

Visitors to the studio are always surprised by how white everything is. Benches of finished petals, ready to become roses, peonies, hellebores — and every single one of them built in undyed white crepe. Colour enters the process last, deliberately, and the piece is better for it.

The reason is simple once seen: colour is a property of shape, not a decoration on it. A living petal is darker at the heart and paler at the edge because light passes through a curve, not because a painter decided so. Dye a petal before you shape it and the gradient sits on the surface like paint. Shape first, then dye, and the gradient lands inside the folds — the shadow of one petal deepening the next, the way it does in the jar on the table.

The dyeing itself is slow and dilute: layer after thin layer, each one decided against the study photographs, each one changing the petal less than you expect and exactly as much as it should. Building white also means mistakes announce themselves early. A badly curved petal is obvious when everything is the same white; hidden in colour, it would get a pass it doesn't deserve.

White is the honest stage of the work. Colour is the reward for getting the shape right.

There is a practical bonus: a white-built piece can wait. Batches in progress sit on the bench at the white stage for days while the rest of the bloom catches up, and nothing fades, nothing dates. The colour is decided once the whole plant exists — which is, if you think about it, how the garden does it too.

One practical note

If you keep a piece at home: dust it with cool air — the cool setting of a hairdryer, held back — and keep it out of direct sun and damp. That is the entire care instruction. The rest is looking.
