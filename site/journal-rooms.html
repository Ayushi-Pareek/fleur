Where Paper Sculpture Belongs in a Room

Ayushi · 5 min read

A sculpture changes a room the moment it arrives, so the real question is not whether it belongs — it is where. Five placements, from the studio's own delivery photographs and collectors' homes, have earned their keep again and again.

The mantel is the classic, and it deserves the reputation. A flower standing at eye-adjacent height, against the long horizontal of a mantel shelf, reads the way a single figure reads in a wide painting. The second is the console or entry table — the first thing a guest meets. A paper rose there does the work of a bouquet, permanently, and starts every visit with the same quiet surprise.

Third: beside the window, in indirect light. Paper takes light beautifully — it is the one material that holds colour the way a petal does, by letting it through — and a sculpture placed where morning light rakes across it changes all day, without any of the wilting. Fourth, the shelf break: a tall stem set among books, breaking the horizontal rhythm of spines the way a thin tree breaks a hedge. And fifth, the bathroom-adjacent mistake worth correcting — humidity is the one enemy, so the powder room down the hall, not the one by the shower.

The placement rule the studio actually follows: give the piece a plain wall behind it, and light that moves. It will do the rest.

What all five have in common is room to be seen alone. A sculpture photographed on a cluttered table looks like clutter; given a metre of clear air and a quiet backdrop, the same piece becomes the reason the room is remembered.

One practical note

If you keep a piece at home: dust it with cool air — the cool setting of a hairdryer, held back — and keep it out of direct sun and damp. That is the entire care instruction. The rest is looking.
