The Rose Pattern

The studio's rose study, adapted for an afternoon at the kitchen table. Print the templates at home, cut along the guides, and assemble petal by petal — plain-language notes walk you through each fold. No specialist skills, and nothing you don't already own.

If you find yourself wanting the real thing — fine crepe, wire, hand-mixed colour — that is The Fleur Method.

Why this experiment

Notes from the worktable

This pattern began as play: an afternoon spent with ordinary printer paper instead of fine crepe, cutting roses just to see how the shapes behave — the tight spiral of a hybrid tea, the loose, many-petalled tumble of a David Austin, the open face of a wild rose. Different papers held the curves differently, and different colour mixes turned the same template into entirely different flowers.

What was learned: a rose is a rhythm problem — petal size, not petal count, decides whether it reads as a rose. What's next: more varieties are being tested at the worktable, and whatever survives the testing joins this shelf. The first one lives on the studio desk in a jar, beside the real thing — which is the whole point of paper.
